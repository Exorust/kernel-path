# Week 2, day 5 · Quantized matvec

**Why this day.** Decode on a Mac is a matrix-vector product over quantized weights, thousands of times per token. It is the kernel llama.cpp was built around, the kernel MLX's `quantized.h` exists for, and the kernel where a ten-line change made news this year.

## The story

**The format.** A 4-bit weight is a small integer plus a scale and an offset shared by a group. MLX's affine mode: "every group_size elements in a row of w are quantized together", with groups of 32, 64, or 128 and 2 to 8 bits, returning the packed weights, the scales, and the biases ([mx.quantize](https://ml-explore.github.io/mlx/build/html/python/_autosummary/mlx.core.quantize.html)). MLX also supports "mxfp4", "mxfp8", and "nvfp4", the block floating-point formats from week 5. llama.cpp's oldest format is simpler: `QK4_0 32`, one half-precision scale, sixteen bytes of nibbles, no bias, 18 bytes per 32 weights ([ggml-common.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/master/ggml/src/ggml-common.h)). Both cut the bytes per weight from 16 bits to about 4.5, which on a memory-bound decode is close to a 3.5x speedup before anyone writes a clever kernel.

**The kernel, and one trick both engines share.** The work per weight is tiny: unpack a nibble, multiply, add. The trick is to never unpack. MLX's `load_vector` pre-scales the activation instead: `x_thread[i + 1] = x[i + 1] / 4.0f; x_thread[i + 2] = x[i + 2] / 16.0f; x_thread[i + 3] = x[i + 3] / 64.0f;`, so the packed bits can be multiplied in place with a mask and no shift, and it keeps a running `sum` of the activations so the per-group bias costs one multiply ([MLX quantized.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ml-explore/mlx/main/mlx/backend/metal/kernels/quantized.h)). llama.cpp's kernel carries the same comment: "we assume that the yl's have been multiplied with the appropriate scale factor that corresponds to the missing bit shifts (1, 1/16, 1/256, 1/4096)", and finishes a block with `d * (sumy * -8.f + acc[0] + acc[1] + acc[2] + acc[3])` ([mul_mv.metal](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/master/ggml/src/ggml-metal/kernels/mul_mv.metal)). Two independent code bases, one idea: move the arithmetic onto the value that is read once.

**Where it stops being easy: small batch.** At one token the kernel is memory-bound and the 4x byte saving is the whole win. Add tokens and each weight is reused M times, so the arithmetic per byte grows and the kernel drifts toward the compute roof. Marlin, the NVIDIA kernel built for this, states the budget: "as long as we perform less than 25-50 (tensor core) multiply-accumulates per 4-bit quantized weight, it should (theoretically) be possible to maintain near ideal 4x speedup over FP16 weights", and delivers "the maximum possible 3.87x speedup up to batchsizes around 16-32" where "prior work" held it to "1-2 tokens" ([Marlin](https://github.com/IST-DASLab/marlin)). The band between batch 2 and batch 32 is where quantized kernels are won or lost, because it is where speculative decoding, tree search, and parallel sampling live.

**The Apple case.** In May 2026 the author of MTPLX, a multi-token-prediction engine on MLX, reported "28 tok/s → 63 tok/s on Qwen3.6-27B on a MacBook Pro M5 Max. 2.24× faster at real temperature 0.6", and named the kernel: "Stock MLX's quantised matrix-vector kernel is tuned for large M (prefill). During MTP verify, M is 3 to 6, one position per draft token. Stock stalls at these shapes. The patch: wider simdgroups, loop unrolling, 10 lines of Metal. Exact, 0.0 diff against stock" ([MTPLX post](https://www.reddit.com/r/LocalLLaMA/comments/1t3zuvy/mtplx_224x_faster_tps_the_native_mtp_inference/)). Read the claim carefully. The 2.24x is the whole pipeline, drafting and verifying together; the ten-line kernel is one of five parts, and the post gives no kernel-only number. What it does show is the shape of the problem: a kernel tuned for M=1 and for large M, with a dead zone between.

MLX then moved. A `qmv_wide` path now exists for small M, gated by `get_qmv_batch_limit`, with the comment "affine qmv_wide only beats qmv on gen-15+". PR #3791, merged 2026-08-08, measured a 70-billion-parameter 4-bit model on an M5 Max: at M=12 the forward went from 294.6 to 241.5 ms, "−18%", and the author tied it to the same use: "which matters for speculative decoding: verification of multi-candidate / tree / long prompt-lookup proposals lands exactly in that window" ([PR #3791](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx/pull/3791)). An unmerged follow-up found the band was not even memory-bound: "At M=32 the op runs at ~92 GB/s, far under the ~400 GB/s roofline, so it's tile/compute-bound with real headroom" ([PR #3863](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx/pull/3863)). A decode kernel that everyone calls memory-bound was, in that band, waiting on its tile shape.

**The director's reading.** A quantized matvec has three regimes: M=1, where bytes are everything; a middle band, where the tile layout decides; and large M, where it is a matmul. Before asking an AI for one, say which regime you are in and which roof you expect to hit. The MTPLX story is what happens when a kernel is only tuned for the ends.
