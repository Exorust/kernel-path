# Week 4, day 2 · Profiling on Apple

**Why this day.** You cannot direct what you cannot see. This page is the honest inventory of what Apple's tools show about a running kernel, what they do not, and which of them an AI can drive for you.

## The story

**The Xcode Metal debugger.** Apple's description: "Debug and profile your Metal workload with a GPU trace", displaying "your Metal workload on a profiling timeline" with "detailed statistics like performance counters and per-line shader profiling data" ([Xcode docs, Metal debugger](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/xcode/metal-debugger)). The counters are the real thing: a 2020 session said "There's over 150 GPU counters to look at", and the Instruments timeline shows "Occupancy, Bandwidth, and limiter counters" under each encoder ([WWDC21 session 10157](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2021/10157/)). Two conditions: "The Performance counters feature is only applicable to Apple GPUs", and "The Metal debugger collects data from the passes running without overlap, so only one pass runs at a time on the device when the profiler measures the performance." That second sentence is why a capture of a whole model is slow and why its timings differ from a real run: the profiler serializes what normally overlaps. The per-line shader cost graph "is available for iOS devices with A17 Pro or later, and Mac computers with M3 or later", so your M5 has it.

**Getting MLX into it.** Two preconditions that the MLX documentation states and people miss: build or run with `MLX_METAL_DEBUG` for source-level labels, and "To capture a GPU trace you must run the application with ``MTL_CAPTURE_ENABLED=1``." Then `mx.metal.start_capture` writes a trace and "You can open and replay the GPU trace in Xcode. The ``Dependencies`` view has a great overview of all operations" ([MLX Metal debugger doc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ml-explore/mlx/main/docs/src/dev/metal_debugger.rst)). Capture one decode step, not a whole generation.

**The programmatic counters.** Outside the GUI, Metal exposes counters through a sample buffer: "A GPU counter is typically a hardware feature that tracks a specific performance metric, such as timestamps before and after an important rendering stage" ([GPU counters docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/metal/gpu-counters-and-counter-sample-buffers)). The common sets are exactly three, `timestamp`, `stageUtilization`, and `statistic`, and "Some GPUs may only support some of the counters within a counter set." One hundred and fifty counters in the GUI, three sets in the API. That gap is the scriptability problem from yesterday in one sentence.

**What changed in 2026.** Two tools close part of it. The Xcode debugger now has a terminal mode: "To investigate GPU traces from the command line, use `gpudebug`, an interactive terminal-based debugger. Because its interface is text-based and self-discoverable, AI agents can run it autonomously to navigate traces, inspect state, and fetch resources to diagnose rendering issues without human intervention." Apple's own instruction for the agent case: "point them at `man gpudebug` and a `.gputrace` file. That's all they need to start investigating autonomously" ([Xcode docs, investigating GPU issues with AI agents](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/xcode/investigating-gpu-issues-with-ai-agents)). The trace tree "has four children: `commands`, `performance`, `api_calls`, and `resources`". And macOS 27 adds a second tool: "On macOS, you can collect the data through metalperftrace, a new command line tool in macOS 27", with "--json to make metalperftrace output in JSON format. Now you get structured output that can be easily fed into scripts for regression testing, or AI agents to automatically triage potential performance issues" ([WWDC26 session 388](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/388/)).

Two cautions. Every example in those documents is a draw call; the tools were designed for games, and whether they report per-compute-dispatch counters was not verified. And "Trace loading and replayer startup can take seconds to minutes depending on the size of the trace", so the loop is minutes, not milliseconds.

**Below the compiler.** There is no Apple disassembler. The community one, dougallj/applegpu, is "reverse engineering the Apple G13 GPU architecture (as used by the M1)" and ships `compiler_explorer.py`, which "Allows you to easily compile, extract, and disassemble shaders" ([applegpu](https://github.com/dougallj/applegpu)). It targets the M1 and may not decode an M5's code. It is the last resort when arithmetic says a kernel's time is impossible and you need to see what the compiler emitted.

**The director's inventory.** For a kernel on your M5 you can get, in order of cost: wall-clock time with an interleaved timer, always; a GPU trace with occupancy, bandwidth, limiter counters and per-line cost, for one dispatch, in minutes; a scripted JSON performance trace on macOS 27; and nothing below the compiler that is guaranteed to work. What you cannot get is the NVIDIA-style one-command, per-kernel, roofline-scored report. So the roofline from week 2 is not a nice-to-have on Apple; it is the substitute for the profiler. Compute the floor, measure the time, and the gap between them is your profile.
