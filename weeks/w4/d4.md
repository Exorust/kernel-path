# Week 4, day 4 · Directing an AI

**Why this day.** Everything before this was knowledge. This is the method: how a person who does not type the kernel gets a correct, fast one out of an AI, with the published cases where it worked and the one where every kernel lost.

## The story

**The case that worked.** In July 2026 a participant in a GPU kernel contest published a worklog: "I placed 12th out of 183 participants, ending up with a 232x speedup over the baseline solution", where "The 232x number above comes from comparing the rough 419,000 µs baseline to the final 1,805 µs tracked result" ([sankalp, autoresearch](https://sankalp.bearblog.dev/autoresearch/)). The agent was Codex, the hardware an NVIDIA B200 rented by the hour. The person's role is described precisely: "I gave inputs every 2-3 hours to move the model in the direction I wanted." "Human in the loop: I would act as the secret sauce to steer the model when it was stuck for long times without improvement." "I gave Modal profiling access to Codex and let it run torch profiling / nsys profiling." The rule they settled on: "Get out of the agent's way you must; steer it back only when it gets stuck." Their program file had a kill rule for abandoned attempts: "Kill a beam only with a clear reason: inherent correctness failure, repeated meaningful regression after a reasonable retune". And the checker was independent of the kernel: "The checker rebuilds Q from (H, tau) and verifies A ≈ QR." Two honest lines close it: "this contest was doable without domain knowledge - like you probably won't make it to the top 10", and "domain expertise accelerates both harness design and human-in-the-loop steering." The expertise went into the harness and the steering, not the code.

**The case where every kernel lost.** From the metalworking site, which summarizes two Apple projects: "DFlash (speculative decoding port, 3.34× total): every hand-written custom kernel in the project lost to stock MLX and was reverted" ([three questions](https://metalworking.vercel.app/war-stories/three-questions/)). The 3.34x came from elsewhere: a change that moved the model's shapes inside the gate of MLX's fused attention. The site's general finding: "the repeated experimental result is that hand-written replacements come back slower", and "Falling off a fast path is silent." The counter-case on the same page is week 2 day 5's MTPLX: "stock quantized matvec stalls at the M=3-6 shapes ... ~10 lines of Metal (wider simdgroups plus unrolling, bit-exact) fixed it." Ten lines, where stock had a hole; zero lines, where stock was already right.

**Three questions before any kernel.** The same page states the order: "Before writing any custom kernel on this platform, ask three questions in order." "Can I delete work? Can I unlock an existing fast path? Can I cut dispatch/sync overhead?" The first is week 2's intensity: fewer bytes or fewer operations beats a faster kernel. The second is week 1 day 4's `mx.fast`: the MLX team's tuned kernels have dispatch gates, and getting your shapes inside a gate is the cheapest 2x on the platform. The third is week 3 day 5. Only after three noes is a custom kernel the answer. An AI will not ask these questions unless the brief does.

**The contract, in MLX's own terms.** Two switches from the custom-kernel documentation belong in every brief. "Passing ``verbose=True`` when calling the kernel ... will print the generated code for debugging purposes": the AI must show the generated function, so you can read the signature it is actually compiling. And "``ensure_row_contiguous`` which is ``True`` by default. This will copy the array inputs if needed before the kernel is launched": a hidden copy that can double a kernel's bytes if the brief does not say what layout the inputs have ([MLX custom kernels doc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ml-explore/mlx/main/docs/src/dev/custom_metal_kernels.rst)).

**The shipped example of a spec-first kernel.** Week 3 day 3's PR #1559 is a brief turned into code. Its description names the shape, the mechanism of the slowness, the change, the contract, and the measurement: one row per layer, "bitwise-identical by construction", a kill switch, and an end-to-end number beside the kernel number. That is the shape of a good brief whether a person or an AI writes the kernel.

**The brief.** Assembled from this week:

1. The shape: batch, sequence, heads, dimensions, and dtype, with the model it comes from.
2. The three questions, answered in writing, with the fast-path gate you checked.
3. The bound: bytes, operations, which roof, and the floor time on your chip, from week 2 day 1.
4. The baseline: the stock MLX kernel for this shape, timed with the interleaved method, not a Python loop.
5. The correctness contract: a slow reference, the tolerance or bit-exactness rule, and test inputs that cover both signs and every dispatch boundary, from day 1 and day 3.
6. The output you want to see: generated source, the measured time against the floor, and the end-to-end number.
7. The kill rule: when to abandon the attempt, from the autoresearch worklog.

Then step back. The worklog's steering interval was hours, not minutes. Your job is the harness and the direction, and tomorrow you write one.

**One more thing the record shows.** MLX's own maintainers do this now. A merged pull request from September 2026 says in its description, "Given #4567 I asked Claude code to do the same for `gather_qmm`", and shipped a 1.17 to 1.44x kernel ([MLX PR #4572](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx/pull/4572)). Another issue is "Posted by Claude (Anthropic's AI assistant) on behalf of" a user. And two other issues carry the opposite line in their template: "I understand it is strictly prohibited to use AI to write issues." The field has not decided. The brief above is how to be on the right side of it: the measurements are yours, the contract is explicit, and the AI's name is on the code, not on the claim.
