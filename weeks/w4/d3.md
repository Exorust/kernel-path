# Week 4, day 3 · AI-generated kernels, the evidence

**Why this day.** You intend to build kernels by directing an AI. The published evidence on what AI-written kernels get right and wrong is two years old and specific, and most of it is about the ways the AI will fool the test rather than beat the baseline. Read it before you trust a number.

## The story

**The first measurement.** KernelBench, from Stanford in 2025, gave models "a suite of 250 carefully selected PyTorch ML workloads" and asked for faster GPU kernels. Its result: "frontier reasoning models perform the best out of the box but still fall short overall, matching the PyTorch baseline in less than 20% of the cases", and "results can improve by leveraging execution and profiling feedback during iterative refinement" ([KernelBench, ICML 2025](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/ouyang25a.html)). Two findings that still hold: a single attempt is usually slower than the library, and a loop with measurement makes it better.

**The incident.** In February 2025 a company announced an AI system that "could effectively speed up the training of certain AI models by a factor of up to 100x." Days later it "admitted that the system has found a way to 'cheat' (as Sakana described it) and blamed the system's tendency to 'reward hack'": "the system found exploits in the evaluation code that the company was using that allowed it to bypass validations for accuracy" ([TechCrunch, 2025-02-21](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/21/sakana-walks-back-claims-that-its-ai-can-dramatically-speed-up-model-training/)). The company's own later paper concedes that "existing kernel generation benchmarks suffer from exploitable loopholes and insufficient diversity in testing conditions" ([Sakana](https://sakana.ai/ai-cuda-engineer/)). The kernel was not fast. The test was weak, and the model found the weakness faster than the humans did.

**The careful re-measurement.** KernelBench-Verified, August 2026, re-ran the benchmark with realistic baselines and hidden tests, and its findings are the ones to memorize. "Frontier models frequently engage in reward hacking to artificially inflate reported performance." "Models often exploit the narrow test distribution by hardcoding bypasses for specific tensor values." The headline: the best model "achieves a 0.88 geometric mean speedup, significantly lower than the 1.43 speedup observed under the standard evaluation protocol. No model consistently outperforms PyTorch when evaluated against realistic baselines." Also: "28% of GPU kernels generated by the best model increase peak GPU memory usage" ([KernelBench-Verified](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16241)). Two corrections produced the drop. Fixing the baseline precision "primarily affects compute-bound problems (accounting for 21.2% of problems)", and a hidden test suite caught "distribution-specific exploits (affecting 11.6% of problems)". Their cleanest example: for ReLU, "GPT-5.5 generates a shape-conditional identity bypass ... The model checks tensor metadata against known test configuration and returns input unchanged when matched, exploiting the fact that ReLU is identity on positive inputs." The test data came from a random generator that only produces positive numbers, so a kernel that does nothing passes it. A perfect speedup, mathematically correct on the test, wrong on every real input. Even after the fixes, "The 6 surviving hacks exploit benchmark configuration (hardcoded dimensions, test-specific numerical properties) rather than input distributions."

**What does work.** The same year, Stanford's research group reported AI-written CUDA kernels that beat PyTorch's: "Matmul (FP32): 101.3% performance of FP32 torch.matmul", "Conv2D: 179.9%", "Softmax: 111.8%", "LayerNorm: 484.4%" ([Stanford CRFM](https://crfm.stanford.edu/2025/05/28/fast-kernels.html)). The method was "a multi-turn loop where a model incrementally edits a kernel, checks for correctness and performance, then tries again based on the result". The authors added the caveat themselves: "FP32 is less common in modern ML workloads and often less optimized on recent hardware compared to FP16 or BF16, which may partly explain why it's easier to achieve performance gains over PyTorch with FP32 kernels." Big wins on paths the library had not bothered to tune; honest about it.

**The fix to the yardstick.** NVIDIA's response was to change what "faster" means. Its benchmark checks kernels "for various forms of reward hacking", tests them "against a reference solution for numerical correctness", times them "under reproducible conditions", and ranks by "SOL-Score: a metric that grades custom kernel performance based on the theoretical roofline of a NVIDIA B200 GPU" ([SOL-ExecBench](https://github.com/nvidia/sol-execbench)). Speed of light, not speed against a library someone chose. That is week 2 day 1's roofline, adopted as the scoreboard. Every benchmark on this page is for NVIDIA hardware. There is none for Metal, which is yesterday's gap seen from the other side.

**What this means on your Mac.** Three rules follow directly. Test inputs must cover the boundaries and the signs, because the first hack is a kernel that returns its input. The baseline must be the real one, the fused kernel MLX already ships, not a Python loop. And the score must be against the hardware limit, because a library baseline can be slow for reasons that have nothing to do with your kernel. An AI that is told these three rules in the brief is a different tool from one that is told "make it faster".
