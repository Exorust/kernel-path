# Week 4, day 1 · The feedback-loop problem on Metal

**Why this day.** On NVIDIA, a kernel writer has a profiler that scores every kernel against the hardware limit, a public benchmark that ranks kernels, and test suites that catch wrong answers. On Apple, parts of that loop are missing or young, and the missing parts are exactly where an AI-written kernel goes wrong without anyone noticing. This week is about building the loop yourself.

## The story

**Wrong answers arrive silently.** The MLX issue tracker is the best public record of how GPU code fails on Apple. A search for the word "silently" in the repository's issues returned 62 results on 2026-10-08. Four of them, in the reporters' own words:

- "`mlx.nn.Conv2d` / `mx.conv2d` on Metal silently returns an all-zero tensor for large batched inputs. No exception, no warning — the op completes and produces zeros" ([issue #3979](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx/issues/3979), August 2026, closed).
- A decode kernel that "collapses into a runaway of `!` tokens starting at exactly the first step whose KV length reaches the 2-pass routing threshold of 1024" ([issue #4022](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx/issues/4022)). The dispatch rule from week 3 day 2, failing at its boundary.
- Custom kernels "silently dispatched over a truncated threadgrid whenever a grid dimension is smaller than the matching requested threadgroup dimension — which is the common case" ([issue #4534](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx/issues/4534), closed September 2026). The grid and threadgroup tuples from week 1 day 4, quietly cut short.
- Softmax and the norms "drop rows when grid width exceeds 2^32", with "The result is silently wrong (no exception and no error), just returning zeros" ([issue #4624](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx/issues/4624), open).

None of these raised an error. The kernel ran, returned a tensor of the right shape, and the numbers were wrong. A test that checks shapes passes. A test that checks a few values passes if the bug lives at a boundary the test does not reach.

**The one that breaks the tool you would reach for.** Issue #4603, open on 2026-10-07: a custom kernel "returns whole wrong output rows on an Apple M3 Ultra running macOS 27.0 (26A428), deterministically", correct on an M2 and correct under a different math mode. The reporter tried Apple's shader validation layer, `MTL_SHADER_VALIDATION=1`, "which reports nothing and perturbs the code generation enough to hide it". To prove where the fault was, they wrote "An MLX-free metal-cpp driver" that "reproduces it from the same text, so this looks like a bug in the OS's runtime Metal compiler rather than in MLX" ([issue #4603](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx/issues/4603)). That is the correctness loop done by hand: a second implementation, a comparison, and a reproducer that strips every layer until the culprit is alone. It is also a warning that the compiler itself can be the bug, and that validation tools can hide what they are meant to find.

**The performance loop.** Apple ships a profiler, the Xcode Metal debugger, with "performance counters and per-line shader profiling data"; tomorrow is about it. What it is not is scriptable and per-kernel in the way NVIDIA's Nsight Compute is. The metalworking site, a secondary source, states the practical complaint: "it's a GUI (no CI, no scripted regression checks), and capture perturbs heavily on big ML workloads, with inference projects reporting 50-100× slowdowns and multi-GB traces. Usable for staring at one dispatch; unusable as a feedback loop" ([metalworking, profiling](https://metalworking.vercel.app/metal/profiling/)). The slowdown figure is that site's and is not independently verified. What is verified is the direction of travel: macOS 27 adds a command-line tool, `metalperftrace`, and the Xcode debugger has a terminal mode that Apple says agents can drive; both are on tomorrow's page.

**The benchmark loop.** NVIDIA has KernelBench and a speed-of-light scoreboard, day 3's subject. The research for this course found no equivalent for Metal, no shared suite of problems that two Apple kernels could be scored against, and no public leaderboard. That absence is not a published complaint anywhere; it is a null result from searching. Its effect is that "faster" on Apple always means "faster than the baseline I chose", which is the week 3 day 3 lesson about PR #1241's 56x.

**How this changed during the course.** When the first version of this course was written in September 2026, MLX's attention had no fused backward on Metal; the source threw "NYI". On 2026-09-29 it merged one. The small-batch quantized matvec gap from week 2 day 5 was narrowed by two merges in the first week of October. The loop on Apple is young and moving, which means two things for you: check any claimed gap on the day you pick it, and expect that a kernel verified on your machine today may need re-verifying after the next macOS.

**What a working loop looks like.** Three parts, each of which week 3's successful kernels had. A reference that is slow and trusted, in float64 or on the CPU. A comparison with a stated contract, either bit-exact like PR #1559's "bitwise-identical by construction" or a numeric tolerance fixed before optimization starts. And a timing method that interleaves the candidate with the baseline so that the laptop's thermal state hits both. The first version of this repository, on the `v1` branch, has a timer that does the third part. Day 4 turns the three parts into the brief you hand an AI.
