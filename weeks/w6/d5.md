# Week 6, day 5 · Profiling, AI kernels, and the second brief

**Why this day.** The last page closes the loop week 4 opened. On NVIDIA the profiler, the benchmark, and the agent loop all exist and are measured. Knowing what they look like when they work is the standard to hold your Apple loop to, and the second brief is written against that standard.

## The story

**The profiler that scores against the hardware.** "Collection of performance metrics is the key feature of NVIDIA Nsight Compute. Since there is a huge list of metrics available, it is often easier to use some of the tool's pre-defined sets or sections" ([Nsight Compute guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/nsight-compute/ProfilingGuide/index.html)). Its first section is named for the idea this course has used since week 2: Speed Of Light, "High-level overview of the throughput for compute and memory resources of the GPU. For each unit, the throughput reports the achieved percentage of utilization with respect to the theoretical maximum." The percentage of the roof, computed by the tool from counters, per kernel, from the command line. Its advice on the next step is the one week 4 day 2 could only approximate on Apple: "Only focus on stalls if the schedulers fail to issue every cycle." And its cost: "In Kernel Replay, all metrics requested for a specific kernel instance in NVIDIA Nsight Compute are grouped into one or more passes", so a kernel is re-run per group of counters, which is why the Marlin authors could say their "PTX assembly was extensively analyzed in NSight-Compute". This is the tool whose absence on Apple makes the roofline your profiler.

**The benchmark, and its correction.** Week 4 day 3 covered KernelBench, "matching the PyTorch baseline in less than 20% of the cases", and its verified sequel's drop from 1.43 to 0.88. NVIDIA's reframing is the end state: "progress is constrained by benchmarks that reward speedup over software baselines rather than proximity to hardware-efficient execution", so "SOL-ExecBench reframes GPU kernel benchmarking from beating a mutable software baseline to closing the remaining gap to hardware Speed-of-Light", with "235 CUDA kernel optimization problems extracted from 124 production and emerging AI models", run in "a sandboxed harness with GPU clock locking, L2 cache clearing, isolated subprocess execution, and static analysis based checks against common reward-hacking strategies" ([SOL-ExecBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19173)). Clock locking, cache clearing, process isolation, and hack detection: four things a trustworthy timing needs that a bare timer lacks. Your Apple timer from the v1 branch has the first two in spirit, interleaving and thermal flags, and none of the last two.

**The loop, measured.** The Stanford search loop produced "101.3%" to "484.4%" of FP32 PyTorch with its FP32 caveat. A 2026 agent loop on an H100 reported "5.29x over eager on RMSNorm, 2.82x on softmax, and 2.21x on cross-entropy, while beating torch.compile by 2.83x, 3.44x, and 2.94x respectively", and "first place on the vectorsum_v2 B200 leaderboard" ([AutoKernel](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21331)); an outside reviewer noted its "matmul is a weak spot at ~28% of cuBLAS throughput". The pattern across every AI-kernel result in this course: large wins on elementwise and normalization kernels that the libraries left on ordinary cores, small or negative results on matmul and attention where the libraries use the matrix units. And one audit from the megakernel contest, via this course's August synthesis: a "19.4x" leaderboard entry "was 9 Triton kernels under a CUDA graph, audited and rejected". The loop works, the scoreboard needs an auditor, and the auditor's tools are the ones on this page.

**The second brief.** Week 4 day 5 was an Apple brief. Write the NVIDIA one, for a rented H100 through a per-second cloud, with the same seven parts and these differences:

1. The layer, from week 5 day 2: Triton for a first version, with the PTX dumped and read; inline PTX only if the brief can name the instruction.
2. The compute capability, from week 5 day 5: 9.0 for an H100; 10.0 or 12.0 are different machines.
3. The bound against the H100's numbers from week 5 day 1: 3.35 TB/s and about 989 dense BF16 TFLOPS, ridge in the hundreds.
4. The baseline: the library kernel, cuBLAS, cuDNN, or FLA, timed under the same clock lock.
5. The contract: the same reference and boundary tests as the Apple brief, plus the four harness properties above, because the hacks in week 4 day 3 were all found on this hardware.
6. The output: the Nsight Speed Of Light section for the kernel, not a wall-clock number alone.
7. The kill rule, and the comparison to Apple: the same kernel's roofline position on your M5, so the brief says which platform the kernel is for and why.

A good candidate is the chunked gated-delta prefill from week 4 day 5 in Triton, measured against FLA's own kernel at 2,048 and 8,192 tokens, because week 6 day 4 gave you FLA's numbers and FlashQLA's benchmark file to check against, and because its Apple twin is your first brief. Two briefs for one kernel on two platforms is the whole course in one document.
